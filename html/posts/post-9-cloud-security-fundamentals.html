Day 7 at ReDI: Cloud Security Fundamentals Made Simple

By Oriana • May 2 2026 • 2 min read • #cloud #security #ReDI

In this session, I learned that cloud security is not only about big companies and complex infrastructure. It is also about the everyday tools we already use, like email, streaming platforms, shared documents, and online banking. That made the topic feel much more real and much easier to understand.

First, What the Cloud Really Means

The cloud is simply servers, storage, and software that are accessed over the internet instead of living only on your own computer. What stood out to me is how normal this has become. Most of us use cloud services every day without even thinking about it.

I also learned why businesses moved in this direction. Instead of buying and maintaining a lot of expensive hardware, they can rent what they need, scale up quickly, and avoid wasting resources.

The Part That Made It Click for Me

The pizza analogy made the different cloud models much easier to follow. On-premise is like making pizza at home: you buy everything and handle everything. IaaS is like renting a kitchen. PaaS is more like using a ready-made food truck setup. SaaS is just ordering the pizza and eating it.

That comparison helped me understand the main difference between the models: how much the provider handles, and how much the customer still needs to manage.

The Biggest Lesson: Shared Responsibility

This was probably the most important part of the session. Even if a cloud provider manages the infrastructure, the customer is still responsible for important things like access control, passwords, permissions, and protecting data.

In other words, using the cloud does not mean security becomes someone else’s job. The provider and the customer both have responsibilities, and problems often happen when that line is misunderstood.

Threats That Felt Very Real

What I liked about this session is that the risks were explained in a practical way. Many cloud problems are not caused by some dramatic Hollywood-style hack. They often come from simple mistakes.

Examples that stayed with me were public storage buckets, weak passwords, missing MFA, exposed API keys, and giving too many permissions to users or apps. These are small decisions that can create very big consequences.

What I Learned From the Case Studies

The case studies made the shared responsibility model much clearer. In many examples, the provider was not the one who failed. The customer made a mistake through bad settings, weak security habits, or poor secret management.

That was a useful reminder that security is often about discipline: checking permissions, limiting exposure, updating dependencies, backing up data, and using least privilege wherever possible.

Virtualization Also Matters

I also learned that virtualization is one of the foundations of cloud computing. It allows one physical machine to run many virtual machines, which saves money and makes cloud platforms flexible.

At the same time, it introduces its own risks, such as outdated virtual machines, insecure snapshots, and poor visibility over what is still running. So even the technology that makes the cloud efficient needs to be managed carefully.

What I’m Taking Away

The main lesson I’m keeping from this session is that cloud security is often about getting the basics right. Use strong authentication, enable MFA, avoid public exposure, encrypt sensitive data, watch permissions, and do not assume the provider protects everything for you.

This session made cloud security feel less abstract and more practical. It showed me that a lot of security comes down to awareness, good habits, and understanding where responsibility really sits.
